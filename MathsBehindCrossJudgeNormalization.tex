\hypertarget{the-maths-behind-normalization}{%
\section{The Maths Behind
Normalization}\label{the-maths-behind-normalization}}

How this platform puts judges who score on different personal scales
onto one common scale --- stated exactly, then proved on the real
\texttt{fixtures.json} data.

\hypertarget{the-problem-measured}{%
\subsection{1. The problem, measured}\label{the-problem-measured}}

\texttt{fixtures.json} holds 126 evaluations of 41 projects by 30
judges, each scored 2--5 on functionality / quality / innovation. The
judges do not share a scale:

\begin{longtable}[]{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule\noalign{}
judge & mean given & spread (sd) & evaluations \\
\midrule\noalign{}
\endhead
\bottomrule\noalign{}
\endlastfoot
jdg\_01 & 2.00 & 0.00 & 1 \\
jdg\_14 & 3.00 & 0.67 & 3 \\
jdg\_27 & 3.00 & 0.47 & 2 \\
\ldots{} & \ldots{} & \ldots{} & \ldots{} \\
jdg\_15 & 4.06 & 0.83 & 6 \\
jdg\_30 & 4.08 & 0.57 & 4 \\
jdg\_02 & 4.22 & 0.81 & 6 \\
\end{longtable}

The strictest judge averages \textbf{2.00}, the most lenient
\textbf{4.22} --- a 2.2-point gap on a 4-point scale. A raw average
confounds \emph{project quality} with \emph{who happened to judge it}: a
project seen only by harsh judges looks bad, one seen by two lenient
judges looks elite. That is the entire problem normalization has to
solve, and averaging cannot solve it by construction.

\hypertarget{the-method-what-the-code-actually-does}{%
\subsection{2. The method (what the code actually
does)}\label{the-method-what-the-code-actually-does}}

Normalization here is not one trick but five stages, each removing one
way a judge's personal scale can leak into the ranking. Code pointers
are exact.

\hypertarget{stage-1-criterion-averaging-score_evaluation-hier_score.py}{%
\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Stage 1 --- Criterion averaging
(\texttt{score\_evaluation},
\texttt{hier\_score.py})}{Stage 1 --- Criterion averaging (score\_evaluation, hier\_score.py)}}\label{stage-1-criterion-averaging-score_evaluation-hier_score.py}}

One evaluation's criterion scores become a single number (mean under
equal weights here --- fixtures define no weights, so shares are
uniform; organizer weights are normalized to sum 1 in production).
Per-criterion scales (\texttt{score\_lo}/\texttt{score\_hi}, migration
\texttt{0016}) map every criterion to 0--1 first, so a 1--5 rubric and a
0--10 rubric are comparable before weighting.

\hypertarget{stage-2-pairwise-conversion-generate_pairs-pairwise.py}{%
\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Stage 2 --- Pairwise conversion
(\texttt{generate\_pairs},
\texttt{pairwise.py})}{Stage 2 --- Pairwise conversion (generate\_pairs, pairwise.py)}}\label{stage-2-pairwise-conversion-generate_pairs-pairwise.py}}

Each judge's scores become \textbf{within-judge pairwise orderings}: for
every pair of projects a judge scored, the higher-scored one wins. This
is where absolute scale dies --- a judge scoring (2, 3, 4) and one
scoring (60, 70, 80) produce the \emph{identical} evidence. Three rules
keep it honest:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Unit weights, always.} Every comparison carries weight 1.0,
  enforced by a database CHECK constraint as well as in code. Score
  \emph{margins} never become evidence weights --- otherwise a judge
  using a wide scale would outvote a careful one purely for being loud.
\item
  \textbf{Ties produce nothing.} Scores within 1e-9 emit no observation
  (the epsilon exists because weights like 0.35 aren't binary-exact;
  crowning a winner on float dust would be fabrication).
\item
  \textbf{Snapshots, not live reads.} Pairs are built from stored
  weighted scores, so later rubric edits can never reinterpret history.
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{stage-3-reliability-weighted-map-fit-fit-crowd_bt.py}{%
\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Stage 3 --- Reliability-weighted MAP fit
(\texttt{fit},
\texttt{crowd\_bt.py})}{Stage 3 --- Reliability-weighted MAP fit (fit, crowd\_bt.py)}}\label{stage-3-reliability-weighted-map-fit-fit-crowd_bt.py}}

Model:
\texttt{P\_j(A\ \textgreater{}\ B)\ =\ sigmoid(r\_j\ ·\ (θ\_A\ −\ θ\_B))},
judge reliability \texttt{r\_j\ =\ exp(log\ r\_j)}, priors
\texttt{θ\ \textasciitilde{}\ N(0,\ 2²)},
\texttt{log\ r\_j\ \textasciitilde{}\ N(μ\_j,\ σ\_j²)} with cross-event
historical or neutral \texttt{(0,\ 0.60)} priors. Fitted by BFGS from
zeros with an analytic gradient.

Two normalizing effects live here. First, \textbf{reliability pools
agreement, not scale}: a judge whose orderings agree with the consensus
gets high \texttt{r} regardless of whether they score 1--5 or 0--100; a
contrarian's \texttt{r} collapses toward 0 and their pairs stop moving
the ranking (demonstrated adversarially in
\texttt{scripts/validate\_ranking.py} experiment 3: inverted judge → r ≈
0.36 vs ≈1.97 for consistent judges). Second, \textbf{the θ prior
shrinks thin evidence}: a project with two evaluations cannot stray far
from 0 no matter its raw scores --- absence of evidence is reported as
modesty, not confidence.

\hypertarget{stage-4-sum-to-zero-reporting-crowd_bt.py-rank}{%
\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Stage 4 --- Sum-to-zero reporting
(\texttt{crowd\_bt.py},
\texttt{rank})}{Stage 4 --- Sum-to-zero reporting (crowd\_bt.py, rank)}}\label{stage-4-sum-to-zero-reporting-crowd_bt.py-rank}}

The BT likelihood depends only on \emph{differences}, so reported thetas
are centered (reference project included in the mean). Rankings, gaps
and probabilities are therefore symmetric and stable across
recalculations.

\hypertarget{stage-5-laplace-uncertainty-same-file}{%
\subsubsection{Stage 5 --- Laplace uncertainty (same
file)}\label{stage-5-laplace-uncertainty-same-file}}

The full inverse-Hessian theta block propagates through the centering
shift --- including the reference project, which is no longer special
after Stage 4 --- giving every project an honest marginal std, pairwise
\texttt{P(A\textgreater{}B)} flags, and High/Low confidence. Stated
assumption: thetas are treated as independent Gaussians for pairwise
probabilities (posterior correlations are dropped; useful as a flagging
heuristic, not calibrated odds).

\hypertarget{the-second-opinion-severity-pooling-hier_score.py}{%
\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{The second opinion: severity pooling
(\texttt{hier\_score.py})}{The second opinion: severity pooling (hier\_score.py)}}\label{the-second-opinion-severity-pooling-hier_score.py}}

Where BT needs pairs, the edge-case Bayesian model
(\texttt{R\ =\ θ\ +\ b\ +\ ε}, EM variance components, v2) estimates an
explicit per-judge severity \texttt{b\_j} with partial pooling --- a
judge seen once collapses to the population instead of absorbing project
spread. It is a different estimator of the same correction, and §4
checks they agree on fixtures.

\hypertarget{theorem-affine-invariance-sketch}{%
\subsection{3. Theorem (affine invariance,
sketch)}\label{theorem-affine-invariance-sketch}}

\textbf{Claim.} Let one judge's scores undergo any strictly increasing
affine map \texttt{x\ ↦\ ax\ +\ b}, \texttt{a\ \textgreater{}\ 0}. Then
the fitted thetas are \emph{exactly} unchanged.

\textbf{Proof.} (i) The map preserves strict order, so
\texttt{generate\_pairs} emits a byte-identical pair set (ties map to
ties: \texttt{ax+b} preserves equality too). (ii) The MAP objective
depends on the data only through that pair set (unit weights, no margins
anywhere), with identical priors, reference, and start point. (iii) BFGS
is deterministic given identical objective/gradient/start, so the
optimum --- and hence every reported theta --- is identical. \ensuremath{\blacksquare}

Corollaries a statistician should verify rather than take on faith: the
claim covers affine maps only (a judge who \emph{reorders} projects
changes the evidence --- correctly so); reliability posteriors are
likewise invariant since they see the same pairs; the θ prior introduces
mild shrinkage but no scale dependence.

\textbf{Affine-invariance check on the same data.} Mapping jdg\_08's
scores 2--5 → 10--100 (\texttt{30x\ −\ 50}, strictly increasing): pair
sets byte-identical, fitted thetas differ by \textbf{0.00e+00} (max over
41 projects), reliabilities unchanged to 4 decimals (jdg\_08 1.1441,
jdg\_19 0.5648, jdg\_12 1.0844). The wider scale bought jdg\_08 exactly
nothing --- as §3 predicts.

jdg\_30 by both raw means and posterior severity \texttt{b}), with

estimators, one agreed correction.

\hypertarget{what-normalization-does-not-do-read-before-citing-this-doc}{%
\subsection{5. What normalization does NOT do (read before citing this
doc)}\label{what-normalization-does-not-do-read-before-citing-this-doc}}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{No cross-component miracles.} Projects sharing no judge path
  sit on no common scale; the pipeline refuses to fit
  (\texttt{connected\_components} gate) rather than invent a ranking.
  Normalization aligns scales, it does not create evidence.
\item
  \textbf{Thin evidence stays modest.} Few evaluations → posterior hugs
  the prior; prj\_10's demotion is the feature working, but it means a
  genuinely great project seen twice cannot outrank a good one seen ten
  times.
\item
  \textbf{Reliability is agreement, not accuracy.} A panel of
  correlated-but-wrong judges (shared misconception, leaked rubric)
  would mutually reinforce. The adversary test covers inversion, not
  collusion.
\item
  \textbf{Reference dependence is mild, not zero.} Sum-to-zero makes
  \emph{reporting} shift-invariant, but the fitted optimum still feels
  the reference through the prior (re-demonstrated in context §42) ---
  second-order, disclosed.
\item
  \textbf{History transfer is mean-only.} Cross-event priors carry
  posterior means with neutral width (MAP has no posterior variance to
  propagate).
\end{itemize}
